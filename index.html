PhD research · 2026 – 2029

Physical AI: letting light do the computing

Neural networks spend most of their energy on matrix multiplications. Light can perform some of these operations as it propagates, in parallel and with very little energy. My PhD explores how to turn that physics into useful computing hardware.

I joined the PICo group of Prof. Sylvain Gigan at the Laboratoire Kastler Brossel in November 2026. The group studies how light behaves in complex media, materials such as frosted glass or biological tissue, where a beam is scattered so many times that it comes out as a grainy pattern called speckle.

That scrambling looks like noise, but it is deterministic. The same input always produces the same speckle, which means a scattering medium acts like a huge fixed random matrix applied at the speed of light. This is the starting point for optical processors that can accelerate machine learning.

My work sits where optics, artificial intelligence and unconventional computing architectures meet. I'll add results and publications here as the project develops.

From the lab to industry

LightOn: optical computing as a company

In 2016, Sylvain Gigan co-founded LightOn in Paris with Igor Carron, Laurent Daudet and Florent Krzakala. Their idea came straight from research on light in complex media: use a scattering material as a physical co-processor for AI.

LightOn's Optical Processing Unit (OPU) encodes data onto a laser beam, sends it through a scattering medium and records the resulting speckle on a camera. The medium multiplies the input by a very large fixed random matrix in a single pass of light. Random projections like this are a building block of many machine learning methods, and the optical version runs them at a size and energy cost that are hard to match electronically.

The company then broadened into large language models and now builds Paradigm, a secure generative AI platform for enterprises. It is a good example of where my PhD field can lead: physics done in an academic lab becoming computing hardware and products used in practice.

Continuous-variable quantum computing

A photonic quantum computer can encode information in the continuous amplitude and phase of light, its quadratures, instead of in single photons. To read those quadratures out you need homodyne detectors that are quieter than the quantum noise of light itself. I built and characterized them.

In balanced homodyne detection, the weak quantum signal is mixed with a strong local oscillator on a 50:50 beam splitter. Two photodiodes measure the outputs and their photocurrents are subtracted. The classical noise of the local oscillator cancels, and what remains is proportional to one quadrature of the signal. Changing the local oscillator phase selects which quadrature you measure.

  • Contributed to the design, alignment and optimization of the balanced detection scheme.
  • Demonstrated shot-noise-limited operation up to 150 MHz, with a common-mode rejection ratio above 50 dB and 18 dB of clearance.
  • Integrated the detectors into the experimental setup and contributed to the resulting publication.

Read the paper on arXiv (2511.04578), also published in New Journal of Physics.

Nokia Bell Labs · Feb – Aug 2026

6G networks and thin-film lithium niobate

Future 6G links will need optical modulators that are faster, smaller and more power-efficient than today's. Thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) is one of the leading platforms. At Nokia Bell Labs I tested next-generation LiNbO₃ photonic chips for high-speed telecom systems.

An electro-optic modulator turns an electrical signal into an optical one. In a Mach–Zehnder modulator the light splits into two arms, the applied voltage shifts the phase in one of them, and the arms recombine. Depending on the phase difference, the output swings between bright and dark.

  • Characterized electro-optic performance through the key metrics: half-wave voltage Vπ, bias drift, insertion loss and extinction ratio.
  • Investigated InP quantum dot lasers for integrated photonic applications.
  • Built Python pipelines for automated data processing, noise filtering and signal extraction, using methods such as Savitzky–Golay smoothing to make measurements more reliable and device characterization faster.

London · Sep – Oct 2025 · City of London × IBM

Quantum computing for portfolio optimization

At a quantum computing hackathon for finance organized by the City of London with IBM, our team, French Qubits, built a hybrid AI and quantum pipeline that picks a portfolio of assets. Classical and quantum machine learning narrow down the market, and a quantum optimization algorithm (QAOA) chooses the final portfolio.

The problem

An investor wants the best return for a given level of risk. If each asset is either in or out of the portfolio, the choice becomes a combinatorial problem: with N assets there are 2N possible portfolios, and exact classical solvers become impractical beyond a few dozen assets.

The classic Markowitz trade-off between return and risk can be written as a quadratic problem over binary variables (a QUBO). Replacing each binary variable xi with a spin, xi = (1 − Zi)/2, turns it into an Ising Hamiltonian. Finding the best portfolio then means finding the ground state of that Hamiltonian, which is exactly what QAOA is designed to approximate.

We went beyond the textbook version and built a cost Hamiltonian with the constraints a real portfolio manager faces.

Our cost Hamiltonian

− Σ μi xiexpected return
+ Σ σij xi xjrisk (covariance)
+ A (Σ xi − B)²pick exactly B assets
+ λl Σ (1 − Li) xiliquidity
+ Σ Ci xifixed transaction costs
+ Σ Mij xi xjmarket impact
xi ∈ {0, 1} says whether asset i is in the portfolio. Each term maps onto Z and ZZ operators on the qubits.

What we built

A five-stage hybrid pipeline

Today's quantum computers can only handle a handful of assets, so most of the work is choosing which assets reach the quantum stage, and giving the quantum algorithm a good starting point.

  1. 1

    Quantum kernel pre-selection

    Each asset is described by features such as returns, volatility and Sharpe ratio, then encoded into a quantum state with a ZZ feature map. The overlaps between these states form a Gram matrix that captures nonlinear similarities between assets. We turned it into a distance matrix, grouped assets with K-means (choosing the number of clusters by silhouette score), and visualized the structure with kernel PCA and t-SNE.

  2. 2

    AI module

    Two classical tools complement the quantum kernel. A graphical lasso estimates the network of partial correlations between stocks from their daily price variations and clusters them. A dilated residual convolutional network is trained on windows of past returns and trading volume to predict next-day returns, acting as a filter for the most promising assets.

  3. 3

    Warm-start state preparation

    QAOA results depend strongly on the initial state, especially for the fermionic variant. We built a circuit of Rx and Ry rotations that prepares an arbitrary target distribution, trained by minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence with SPSA and with a genetic optimizer. We used it to load the classical solution as a starting point, and to prepare Dicke states that already contain exactly the number of assets we want.

  4. 4

    QAOA and its variants

    We implemented standard QAOA alongside several variants: a fermionic XX + YY mixer that keeps the number of selected assets fixed, a cat-map mixer, multi-angle QAOA with independent angles per qubit, and a Fourier parameterization of the angles. We compared optimizers including COBYLA, SPSA, Nelder–Mead and a custom genetic optimizer.

  5. 5

    Noisy hardware simulation and analysis

    We ran the pipeline on a simulator with a noise model built to mimic IBM quantum hardware, then analyzed the resulting portfolios. We also tested it on a cryptocurrency portfolio (Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana) under different budget, cost and liquidity settings.

Classical baselines

To judge the quantum results fairly, we also implemented classical methods: a Bayesian Black–Litterman model that combines market equilibrium returns with an investor's views, classical solvers for the same problem, and variational quantum imaginary time evolution (V-QITE) as another quantum approach.
